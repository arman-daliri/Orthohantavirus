Orthohantaviruses usually live and circulare in rodents as their natural hosts without causing clinical signs of disease, however, they can spill over and cause infections in humans [1], [2], [3]. Out of 35 orthohantavirus species, a minimum of 21 is known to be pathogenic to humans [3]. To understand how these viruses change, which specific genetic segments cause the disease, and potentially become more harmful, studying reassortment appears to be a promising avenue of research [1]. 
Segment reassortment plays a pivotal role in segmented viruses, since it provides the opportunity for the genome of such viruses to become more infectious and adapt to the new animal hosts [1].
Rizvanov et al study suggests that for a Hantavirus reassortant to survive, especially for distantly related ones, S and L segments should mostly remain from same viral parent. Moreover, M segment compatibility from another pair is needed since it expresses the glycoproteins responsible for cell-surface attachment; inheriting an M segment from a highly infectious parent (like Andes virus (ANDV)) can increase its infectivity. Beyond genetic features, a reassortant's survival is limited by ecological niche overlap and the vulnerability of the host animals to get infected with two different parental strains of virus [1].
Orthohantaviruses predominantly infects microvascular endothelial cells (ECs) in various organs (lungs, heart, kidney, liver, and spleen) and increased vascular permeability is its key driver paired with dysregulated immune responses. Notably, each orthohantavirus species has its specific degree of pathogenicity in different organs. Lack of advanced containment equipments both in vitro and in vivo makes unravelling the pathogenesis of orthohantavirus challenging whether for reservoir hosts or patient-based research [2]. By foreseeing potential reassortants, able to act as a biological threat we may prevent or decrease the fatality and severity rate to a noticeable degree.
The Orthohantavirus spreads between host rodents mainly when they breathe in dried urine particles, but it can also spread through wounds if infectious virus is present in their saliva. Humans catch the virus by inhalation of aerosolized excreta from carrier rodents in endemic areas [2], [3]; however, limited Interhuman transmission has been reported for Andes orthohantavirus (ANDV). Since humans do not easily pass the virus to others, they are considered as dead-end hosts [2].
Repeated epidemic peaks and fluctuating fatality rates (averaging 2.10%) observed in regions like Baoji, in China, highlights the critical need for continuous surveillance of orthohantaviruses in rodents and humans, and encourage anticipating the emergence of novel variants. One main reason for periodic high prevalence is the continual spillover from rodents to humans. The same study revealed by phylogeny that high genetic diversity among circulating orthohantaviruses, including two novel Dabieshan orthohantavirus (DBSV) lineages of unknown pathogenicity co-circulating with Orthohantavirus hantanense (Hantaan virus, HTNV) in the same region. Moreover, HTNV sequences from human patients and rodents shared 96.2–100% nucleotide identity, suggesting direct and continual transmission from rodents to humans. Therefore, identifying the key genetic features of wild-type, non-lab-passaged sequences, along with the reassortment potential and human risk of descendant strains, could provide a vital tool for public health surveillance [4].
Concerning the highly conserved nature of the HTNV terminal sequences, couple of missing bases can be artificially added to the end of the sequences when subscribing to the GenBank [3], therefore only viruses with full S, M, and L segments were kept, following ICTV guidelines and recent orthohantavirus study that base phylogenetic analysis, recombination detection, and lineage classification on coding-complete tri-segment genomes to ensure accuracy and avoid bias from incomplete data [3], [5].
Since HTNV variants circulating in nature can differ by only a few amino acids yet form distinct genetic lineages associated with human infection and altered clinical presentation [3], it is important to develop approaches that predict which emerging variants may pose a risk to human health.
Orthohantaviruses are genetically diverse, prone to emergence of new variants and characteristically restricted by geographic boundaries, a rule to which HTNV is no exception. For this reason, posing a threat to public health while difficult to isolation from both cell culture and patients, the latter is even harder than isolating from rodent samples. Consequently, few HTNV variants have been confirmed from patients, and the clinical characteristics of HFRS caused by HTNV in the studied area remain poorly understood. Given the atypical presentation observed, laboratory diagnosis is essential [3].

Spillover refers to the transmission from the natural reservoir hosts to a different species, encompassing both rodent-to-rodent and rodent-to-human events. While orthohantavirus spillovers to other rodent species is common, it typically leads to the clearance of the virus rather than lasting infection in the new host. Humans are considered as dead-end hosts; however, rare person-to-person transmissions in South American orthohantavirus, Andes virus (ANDV) has been reported [6]. Understanding these transmission dynamics is essential for assessing how orthohantaviruses spread within animal populations and their potential impact on human health.
Rodent subfamilies Murinae, Arvicolinae, Sigmodontinae and Neotominae carry the pathogenic orthohantaviruses. Two disease syndromes are recognised as being associated with orthohantaviruses: haemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) and hantavirus (cardio) pulmonary syndrome (HPS). The fatality rate of HFRS ranges from in PUUV and SAAV <1%[6], [7] to as high as 15% in DOBV[7], depending on the causative orthohantavirus species [6], whereas HPS has a much higher case fatality rate, reaching to 30-60% in some countries [6], [8]. However, determining the case fatality for each HPS-causing species remains difficult due to the rarity of HPS in certain countries [6]. 
The occurrence of these diseases is correlated with the geographical distribution of specific orthohantaviruses rodent reservoir hosts. For example, the Hantaan virus (HTNV) is found in field mice in Asia, and the Sin Nombre virus (SNV) careed by deer mice in North America. Notably, climate change, deforestation and urbanization change rodent habitat and increase the risk of outbreaks. The lack of globally accessible vaccines and challenge of early diagnosis because of unknown nonspecific initial syndromes endanger public health and must be confronted if it is to be mitigated [6].
Human activity can create contact zones between previously separated viral lineages, like how nearly over the past 500 years, Rattus rats spread worldwide, carrying SEOV with them [6]. Such host dispersal may increase opportunities for co-infection and reassortment, although reassortment has not been documented as a direct outcome of these movements. 
Taxonomy provides a standardized means to keep track of an ever-expanding knowledge base of viruses [6].
Orthohantaviruses possess tripartite genomes (S, M, L segments) encoding the nucleocapsid protein, glycoproteins Gn/Gc, and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, respectively [9], [10], [11]. Natural reassortment events have been documented in HTNV, SEOV, PUUV, and other orthohantaviruses, with segment-specific preferences: HTNV favors M or S segment exchange, while SEOV prefers L or M segment reassortment [11], [12]. The L segment appears particularly prone to reassortment in HTNV, with confidence values exceeding 0.9 in detected events [11].
Machine learning has been applied to predict reassortment outcomes in influenza A viruses using nucleotide composition features and intersegment constraints [13], and to predict zoonotic potential from orthohantavirus sequences using tools like VirPipe [14]. However, translating these approaches to orthohantavirus reassortant risk assessment faces fundamental biological barriers: reassortment requires genetic compatibility between parental strains in the form of conserved packaging signals and maintenance of RNA–protein interactions [15], and most reassortment events yield progeny less fit than either parent [16]. Critically, it is not currently possible to predict whether a rodent-borne orthohantavirus will cause human disease, nor to predict disease severity from gene sequences [6].
Successful replication requires the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (encoded by the L segment) to interact physically with the Nucleoprotein (NP, encoded by the S segment) [5]. Research has confirmed that an N-RdRp interaction is required for viral RNA synthesis [7]. The NP must recognize and bind specifically to viral RNA sequences, particularly the "panhandle" structures at the ends of the segments, to encapsidate the genome [6], [7]. Compatibility rules in ML classifier should account for whether a specific lineage's NP can effectively package another lineage's RNA segments [1].
Human infection begins when the viral Glycoproteins (Gn and Gc, encoded by the M segment) bind to specific receptors on human cells, such as beta-3 integrins or Protocadherin-1 [6], [7] Mutations in the M segment that alter these protein-protein interactions directly change the virus's "key," determining its ability to unlock human endothelial cells [1].
Pathogenicity is often driven by the virus’s ability to shut down the human immune response. For example; the NP binds to human importin alpha proteins, preventing the movement of immune-signaling molecules into the nucleus. The Gn cytoplasmic tail interacts with host TRAF3, disrupting the formation of complexes needed to trigger the interferon (antiviral) response. By identifying lineage-defining signature amino acids at these interaction sites (e.g., L179 on NP or V71 on GP), model can learn which specific molecular shuffles lead to hypercytokinemia (the "cytokine storm" observed in fatal cases) [6].
The final stage of the viral life cycle is viral assembly and structural integrity, which is complex and between multiple interactions; 
1- The ribonucleoprotein complex (RNA + NP) must interact with the Gn/Gc glycoproteins to bud into the Golgi apparatus and form a new infectious particle [5], [6].
2- The glycoproteins must form a specific surface lattice to protect the virus and facilitate fusion with the next host cell [6] the model must recognize that mutations which disrupt these structural protein-protein interactions will result in a non-viable virus, regardless of its theoretical pathogenicity [1].
Step 1: Preprocessing (before ML): 
Per-segment alignment (S, M, L) with ClustalW [27] in MEGA X [26] was used for preprocessing and pairwise identity analysis in BioEdit, providing baseline similarity metrics before downstream feature extraction.
Step 1-2. MAFFT + IQ-TREE (Phylogenetic Tree Inference) ──> this project's own pipeline (`all_hanta.py --phylogenetics`) uses IQ-TREE's ModelFinder to select the best-fit evolutionary model per alignment, rather than hardcoding one
    • MAFFT [17]: Used first to perform the Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA). It is exceptionally fast and handles large datasets of long viral segments (like the ~6.5 kb L segment) far more accurately than older tools like ClustalW.
    • IQ-TREE [18] with ModelFinder [19] and ultrafast bootstrap [20]: Used second to build the phylogenetic trees (`iqtree -m MFP -B <bootstrap> --seqtype DNA`). Three independent trees are built (S, M, L); comparing their topologies (dendropy [24] Robinson-Foulds distance, with Bio.Phylo [25] parsing the trees) identifies topological incongruence, a signal of historical reassortment.
    • GTR+Γ+G model [21], [22]: A common ModelFinder selection for viral coding sequences with unequal base frequencies and site-to-site rate variation — GTR (Tavaré, 1986) with Γ-distributed rate heterogeneity (Yang, 1994). Tian et al. (2020) [4] independently found GTR+Γ+G to be the optimal S-gene model using MEGA 6.0 [28], not IQ-TREE — corroborating evidence for the model family, not the source of this project's IQ-TREE-based methodology.
Step 2: Reassortment detection (parallel to ML):
Per-segment maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees (1,000 bootstraps) combined with RDP5 [23] recombination screening (7 methods, P < 0.01, ≥4-method consensus). Phylogenetic incongruence between segment trees plus RDP5 events catalog natural reassortment in our dataset, providing positive training labels for the ML classifier and a benchmark to score whether generated reassortants resemble events observed in nature. Expected outcome: a validated set of natural reassortment events and clade-compatibility rules.
Step 3: Human-infectivity and severity scoring (after ML):
Lineage-defining signature amino acids on NP and GP (e.g., L179 NP; V71, G700, D967 GP) and T-cell epitope conservation serve as diagnostic markers and classifier features. Patient-derived genomes with documented clinical outcomes anchor the harm scale. Expected outcome: for each candidate reassortant, (i) a natural-plausibility score, (ii) a human-infection probability with identifiable marker residues, and (iii) a numeric severity score.

The attached **Orthohantavirus** collection supports a reporting framework that covers data acquisition, reassortment feasibility, zoonotic potential, pathogenicity features, and phylogenetic integration, but the literature provided is much stronger on **genome completeness, phylogenetics, surveillance, host ecology, and segment reassortment patterns** than on exact receptor-docking markers or a validated universal pathogenicity score  (Kuhn et al., 2023; Laenen et al., 2019; Erdin et al., 2024; Fabbri et al., 2025).

## Pipeline Logic

A defensible pipeline should start by keeping only **coding-complete or near-complete genomes** from single isolates, because segmented-virus phylogenetics and taxonomy are weakened when segment records are incomplete or mixed across isolates  (Kuhn et al., 2023; Laenen et al., 2019).

For hantavirids specifically, several studies build phylogenetic analyses from complete or concatenated S, M, and L data, or explicitly note that complete genomes improve inference on geography, diversity, and public-health surveillance  (Goodfellow et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024; Erdin et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025).

Because the user asked for a process, the workflow is best treated as a staged pipeline from retrieval to ML-ready matrix.

## Python Skeleton

Below is a **reporting-grade skeleton** rather than a turnkey script, because the literature here supports the workflow logic more strongly than exact NCBI field names.

```python
import re
import json
import subprocess
from pathlib import Path
import pandas as pd

LAB_PATTERNS = re.compile(r"(passage|passaged|cell culture|vero|adapted|mouse brain|lab strain)", re.I)
VACCINE_PATTERNS = re.compile(r"(vaccine|vaccinal|attenuated)", re.I)

def run(cmd):
    out = subprocess.run(cmd, shell=True, check=True, capture_output=True, text=True)
    return out.stdout

def exclude_record(text):
    text = text or ""
    return bool(LAB_PATTERNS.search(text) or VACCINE_PATTERNS.search(text))

def norm(x):
    x = (x or "").lower().strip()
    x = re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9]+", "_", x)
    return x.strip("_")

def infer_segment(row):
    text = " ".join([str(row.get(c, "")) for c in row.index])
    if re.search(r"\bS[- ]?segment\b", text, re.I): return "S"
    if re.search(r"\bM[- ]?segment\b", text, re.I): return "M"
    if re.search(r"\bL[- ]?segment\b", text, re.I): return "L"
    return None

def build_isolate_key(row):
    parts = [
        norm(row.get("strain") or row.get("isolate")),
        norm(row.get("host")),
        norm(row.get("country")),
        norm(row.get("collection_date"))
    ]
    return "|".join(parts)

df = pd.read_csv("ncbi_orthohantavirus_metadata.csv")
df["free_text"] = df.fillna("").astype(str).agg(" ".join, axis=1)
df = df[~df["free_text"].map(exclude_record)].copy()
df["segment"] = df.apply(infer_segment, axis=1)
df = df[df["segment"].isin(["S", "M", "L"])].copy()
df["isolate_key"] = df.apply(build_isolate_key, axis=1)

grouped = (
    df.groupby(["isolate_key", "segment"], as_index=False)
      .first()
      .pivot(index="isolate_key", columns="segment", values="accession")
      .reset_index()
)

complete = grouped.dropna(subset=["S", "M", "L"]).copy()
assert not complete.empty, "No complete S/M/L isolate trios found"

complete.to_csv("complete_tripartite_isolates.csv", index=False)
```

## ML Matrix

The final matrix should use **one row per isolate-trio**, because reassortment, host association, and phylogenetic incongruence are properties of the linked S/M/L set rather than a single accession  (Kuhn et al., 2023; Goodfellow et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024).

Recommended columns are: isolate_key, accession_S, accession_M, accession_L, virus_name, host_species, reservoir_status, country, region, year, human_case_link, syndrome_type, fatality_known, S_clade, M_clade, L_clade, tree_incongruence_flag, RDP5_signal, segment_identity_S, segment_identity_M, segment_identity_L, completeness_flag, lab_exclusion_flag, vaccine_exclusion_flag, and composite_pathogenicity_score  (Ortiz et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025).

In table form, the strongest evidence supports a pipeline that prioritizes **complete single-isolate triplets**, uses **segment-wise phylogenetics plus reassortment detection**, treats **S/L compatibility as stricter than M**, and encodes **zoonotic and pathogenicity features as composite metadata fields** rather than as a single sequence-only predictor  (Kuhn et al., 2023; Tscherne et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025; Fabbri et al., 2025).

### Consensus from Zotero 02:
# S/L **Compatibility**, Segment Grouping, and **Pathogenicity** Scoring

The literature supports **strict S–L functional coupling**, **isolate-level S/M/L grouping**, and **composite pathogenicity scoring** rather than any single marker  (Tscherne et al., 2025; Kuhn et al., 2023; Noack et al., 2020).

## Reassortment Constraints

| Question | Evidence-based answer | Key supporting evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Does reassortment between **S and L** require strict NP–RdRp compatibility? | **Mostly yes.** Experimental reassortment studies were usually successful for **M-segment exchange**, whereas reassortants disrupting the native **S+L pairing** were often unstable, which supports a strong compatibility requirement between **NP** from S and **RdRp** from L  (Tscherne et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025). | Natural and experimental work both point the same way: heterologous exchange was “only successful” for M in some systems, instability suggested a **co-requirement of L and S from the same species**, and RdRp must interact with encapsidated RNA and nucleocapsid for replication/transcription  (Tscherne et al., 2025). |
| Is this rule absolute? | **No.** S- or L-involving reassortment has still been observed in some hantaviruses, including HTNV, SEOV, and PUUV, so the rule is strong but not absolute across all species  (Park et al., 2025). | HTNV surveillance detected reassortant candidates with **heterogeneous L segments**, showing S–L or M–L incongruence at high confidence  (Park et al., 2025). |
| Why is **M** more exchange-tolerant? | The evidence suggests **M is more permissive** or can provide a selective advantage when reassorted, whereas **S and L** need tighter genetic matching for the replication machinery  (Park et al., 2025; Laenen et al., 2019). | Stable reassortants reported in vitro commonly preserved a matched **L+S backbone** and swapped **M**, such as BCCV L/S with SNV M, SNV L/S with ANDV M, and PHV L/S with PUUV M  (Tscherne et al., 2025). |

## S/M/L Grouping Logic

| Grouping rule | Practical logic | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | Use **one row per viral isolate**, not per accession, and require exactly one **S**, one **M**, and one **L** segment linked to the same isolate  (Goodfellow et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024; Fabbri et al., 2025). | Orthohantaviruses have **tripartite genomes**, so segment-level analysis without isolate linkage can create false biological combinations  (Goodfellow et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024). |
| Completeness filter | Keep only **coding-complete** or near-complete genomes when possible  (Kuhn et al., 2023; Laenen et al., 2019; Goodfellow et al., 2024). | Classification and robust phylogeny are weakened when genomes are fragmented or partial  (Kuhn et al., 2023; Laenen et al., 2019; Bellomo et al., 2025). |
| Same-isolate constraint | Prefer S, M, and L sequences derived from the **same isolate**, not mosaics assembled from different times or places  (Kuhn et al., 2023). | Mixed-isolate segment sets can distort taxonomy, phylogeny, and reassortment inference  (Kuhn et al., 2023). |
| Segment detection | Assign segment by annotation or gene content: **S→NP/NSs**, **M→GP precursor**, **L→RdRp**  (Goodfellow et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024). | This is the cleanest way to normalize inconsistent database labels across records  (Goodfellow et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024). |
| Phylogenetic step | After grouping complete trios, align each segment separately and also analyze concatenated or whole-genome relationships  (Goodfellow et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025). | Reassortment is detected from **between-segment incongruence**, so you need segment-specific trees from the same grouped isolates  (Park et al., 2025; Ortiz et al., 2025). |

## Pathogenicity Scoring

| Feature domain | Suggested ML feature | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical severity | Ordinal score from **0 to 4** based on no known human disease, mild human disease, HFRS, severe HFRS, or HCPS/fatal association  (Noack et al., 2020; Bellomo et al., 2025). | Disease severity varies by virus species, with HFRS and HCPS as distinct major syndromes; some strains are linked to fatal cases  (Noack et al., 2020; Vial et al., 2023; Bellomo et al., 2025). |
| Human outcome markers | Add binary or numeric fields for **fatal case link**, **AKI**, **thrombocytopenia**, **liver injury**, and **respiratory involvement**  (Liu et al., 2024). | These markers capture clinically observed variation among human infections better than one label alone  (Vial et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Noack et al., 2020). |
| Molecular evidence | Include **segment lineage**, **tree incongruence**, **reassortment flag**, and divergence or identity to known pathogenic lineages  (Bellomo et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025). | Pathogenic strains are often identified by clustering with known human-pathogenic clades or by divergence from them  (Bellomo et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2020; Goodfellow et al., 2024). |
| Ecological risk | Add host-related variables: **reservoir species**, host range breadth, rodent prevalence, geography, and human-contact context  (Ortiz et al., 2025; Obando-Rico et al., 2022). | Human risk is strongly shaped by reservoir identity, host distribution, circulation intensity, and environmental conditions  (Vial et al., 2023; Ortiz et al., 2025; Fabbri et al., 2025). |
| Spillover evidence | Add binary fields for **human case detected**, **rodent–human phylogenetic linkage**, and **multi-host circulation**  (Tian et al., 2020; Goodfellow et al., 2021; Goodfellow et al., 2025). | These features capture realized zoonotic transmission rather than only theoretical risk  (Tian et al., 2020; Forbes et al., 2018; Goodfellow et al., 2025). |

**Figure 3:** Clinical, molecular, and ecological features for an ML-ready pathogenicity score

A practical conclusion is that **S and L usually behave as a co-adapted replication module**, **S/M/L grouping should be isolate-level and complete**, and **pathogenicity should be scored as a composite feature set**, not inferred from any single segment alone  (Tscherne et al., 2025; Kuhn et al., 2023; Ortiz et al., 2025).
